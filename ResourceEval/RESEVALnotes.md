- Revealed preference method 
draw statistical inferences on values from acual choiices people make within markets.
can be used in environmental economics to reveal values for non-market goods which are embedded in observed prices of market goods.

In general with the revealed preference methods we have:

1. travel cost
2. hedonic pricing
3. averting behaviour
4. defensive expenditure

all of them use the common feature of using market information and/or behaviour to infer the economic value of an associated non-market impact.

- **Averting behaviour**
Estimates values by examining behavioural changes and actions undertaken
by individuals to avoid or reduce the exposure of environmental risks.

- **Defensive expenditure**
Estimates the value of environmental benefits by analysing the expenditures by indivituals to protect themselves against environmental risks.

- **Hedonic pricing**
Estimates the value of specific environmental attributes (_e.g._ air quality, or green spaces)
on property prices.

- **Travel cost method**
Is very often used to estimate the recreational values of natural areas,
parks and other environmental resources.
By analyzing the cost individual incur to visit the sites.
The behaviour that is being observed is the amount of **money and time** that a person is
willing to spend to travel to a recreational site.
The site is generally an unpriced good for which the market is missing, but other goods and
services used to reach the site and have the recreational experience are market goods with
prices.
The TMC is based on the idea that the cost of travel is a proxy for the value of the
recreational area people visit.
TCM assumes weak complimentarity: if site is too expensive for an individual (no visits)
changes in the condition and availability of the site do not affect the indivitual's utility.
TCM cannot elicit non-use values regarding the site.

## TMC theoretical basis
$u$ utility  
$x$ consumption of composite commodity  
$r-n$ visits to a recreational site  
$q$ site quality  
$m$ non-labour income  
$w$ wage rate  
$tw$ time spent working  
$c$ round trip cost of visit  
$tr$ time required for a return trip to the site  

the individual maximizes:
$u(x,r,q)$
subject to
$m+wt_w=x+cr$ and $t=t_w+t_rr$

substituting the last one in the second last gives:

$$m+wt=x+(c+wt_r)r$$

Define the price of the trip as:
$$p=c+w_{tr}$$

the demand curve for the number of trips is:
$r=r(m,w,p,q)$

## Three models

**The zonal TCM model (Z-TMC)**
- define as a set of _i_ concentric zones surrounding the recreational site of interest.
- Collect information on the annual number of visitors from each zone by means of a survey.

**The individual TCM model (I-TCM)**
- I-TCM model uses data generated by a survey of individuals either at a site or in their home
(individual's use of a site over the past 12 months)
- GIS software is used to determine the distance to site, which can be done for substiture sites too.
Respondents asked about number of visits, socio economic characteristics.

**The pooled TCM method**
- Some projects requiring evaluation concern changes in quality - cleaning up a fishing lake, for example.
- Given strong assumptions, the pooled travel cost model can ve used to estimate the value of changes
in site quality (NOT the same for Z-TCM and I-TCM).

The regression equation for the zonal model
$v_{zj}=V(TC_{zj},Pop_{z},S_{z}),\quad z=1,\cdots,Z$
where:
$V_{zj}$ are visits from zone z to site j
$TC_{zj}$ is the generalized travel cost from zone z to site j
$Pop_z$ is the population of zone z
$S_z$ are socioeconomic variables such as income averaged for each zone z

The dependent variable is often expressed as $\frac{V_{zj}}{Pop_z}$ or trips to site _j_ per capita.

## Regression models
##x simple linear regression model

$Y=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1X}+\epsilon$, where $\epsilon$ is the error term
$Y=\alpha+\beta_x+\epsilon$, where $\epsilon$ is the error term

Multiple linear regression model:
$$Y=\beta_0+\beta_1 X_1+\beta_2 X_2+\cdots+\beta_n X_n+\epsilon$$
where:
_Y_ is the dependent variable being predicted.
$\beta_0$ is the (Y) intercept or constant term.
$\beta_1\beta_2,\cdots,\beta_n$ are the coefficients associated with each independent variable
$X_1,X_2,\cdots,X_n$ respectively, representing their impact on _Y_.
$\epsilon$ is the error term accounting for the difference between the predicted and actual values of _Y_.

## Ordinary Least Square (OLS)
Is a statistical technique used to estimate the
unknwown parameters of a simple linear regression model.

its main objective is to find the regression line that vest fits the observed data by minimizing the **sum of the squared residuals** 

this method estimates the coefficients $\beta_0$ and $\beta_1$ by minimizing the following
objective function:
$$\min_{\hat{\beta}_0, \hat{\beta}_1} \sum_{i=1}^n (Y_i-\hat{\beta}_0-\hat{\beta}_1X_i)^2$$
the estimated regression line is therefore:
$$\hat{Y}_1=\hat{\beta}_0+\hat{\beta}_1X_i$$
The OLS method chooses the regression line that minimizes the sum
of squared residuals.
### Zonal travel cost model: procedure
1. define a set of _i_ concentric zones surrounding the recreational
site of interest.
2. collect info on the annual number of visitors from each zone by means of a survey.
3. calculate the visitation rate by dividing the number of visits arising from each zone by the population of that zone.
4. using a standard value per unit distance travelled and a standard value per unit.
of time, calculate the return trip travel cost from each zone.
5. estimate a regression equation linking the visitation rate _(v)_ to travel costs _(c)_.
_i.e._ $v_i=\alpha+\beta c_i$
6. use the equation to predict visitation rates with different hypothetical entrance
fees, _e.g._ starting with £10: $\hat{v}_i=\alpha+\beta(c_i+10)$
7. calculate total **visits** of numbers by multiplying the predicted visitation rate by
the zonal population and the sum across all _i_ zones yielding a point on
a demand curve.
8. employ the same procedure to evaluate the effect of imposing various
other hypothetical admission charges _e.g._ £20;£30;£40 and £50, _etc_.
to identify additional points on the demand curve.
9. the final step is to estimate the total economic benefits of the site by calculation the area
under the curve.
